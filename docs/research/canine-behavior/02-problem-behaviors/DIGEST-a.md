## 1. Per-source digests

### Guidance / position documents

**[2] Hammerle M, Horst C, Levine E, Overall K, Radosta L, Rafter-Ritchie M, Yin S. 2015 AAHA Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines. JAAHA 51(4):205-221. DOI 10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6527. (read in full, 17 pp)**
- Design: task-force consensus guideline (CEVA/Virbac/AAHA Foundation grant; says it mixes cited evidence with "practical clinical experience and consensus of expert opinion").
- Key content:
  - Referral: "Referral to a veterinary behaviorist **may be recommended** in cases involving self-injury, aggression, multiple concurrent behavioral diagnoses, profound phobias, or for patients not responding to conventional treatment... Dogs either inflicting deep bites or those injuring immunocompromised individuals **should be referred**. Under no circumstances should aggression or any condition involving a clinical diagnosis be referred to a trainer for primary treatment." Trainer is for "normal but undesired" (jumping), "unruly" (pulling), basic manners.
  - Table 5 (behaviors in social maturity that "should prompt veterinary assessment"): fear/withdrawal, any aggression, stereotypies, elimination changes, new fears/phobias, **distress when left alone**, increased reactivity, big activity change.
  - Senior monitoring at least yearly from 5-8 y (larger breeds) or 8-10 y (smaller); senior flags include onset of aggression in a previously non-aggressive dog, disorientation, house soiling, new phobias.
  - Table 1 is an **unranked** list of nonspecific anxiety signs (panting, lip/nose licking, yawning, trembling, freezing, pacing, hiding, scanning, lowered posture, appetite change, ritualized behavior, etc.). It has no low/medium/high tiers, no whale-eye, no growl/snap rungs.
  - "Avoidance and safety are the cornerstones"; "Protection is the first treatment step"; flooding "never recommended" (learned helplessness); aversives opposed because they "often suppress signals of impending aggression, rendering any aggressive dog more dangerous".
  - Forcing fearful puppies/dogs into more exposure sensitizes them; "Patients do not outgrow pathologic fear"; "10% of puppies that were fearful during a physical exam at 8 wk were also fearful at 18 mo" (wording is ambiguous: of all puppies, or of the fearful ones).
  - Separation anxiety "should be considered a behavioral emergency"; clomipramine/fluoxetine "first-line at the earliest sign of clinical distress" with behavior modification. Noise phobia: as-needed anxiolytics are "first line". Sample SRP case: avoid leaving dog alone, relaxation training first, DS/CC only after calm learned behaviors, owner videos, recheck every 2-4 wk until stable then every 3-6 mo.
  - Aggression: "management may be lifelong"; "never cured but can be treated and managed". Basket muzzles: teach voluntary nose-in with rewards; "muzzles can become weapons" if the dog is distressed; spreadable/food treats contraindicated for dogs that become aggressive around food. Medication effect onset can take 5-8 wk.
  - Numbers: 78.5% (106/135) of dogs fearful on the exam table (single older study).
- Limitations: consensus, industry-funded, much of it written for clinic staff.
- Supports: R-REF-02/03/06/07/08, R-TRI-02, R-MGT-01/05, R-MED-01, R-DSCC-04, referral-gate "aggression never to a trainer". Contradicts/qualifies: R-REF-01 "age 7" (AAHA gives size-dependent 5-8/8-10 and calls them monitoring ages, not referral ages), R-TRI-01 (AAHA Table 5 and the SRP "emergency" framing are stricter than "mild separation protest is engine-eligible"), NOTES 1.6 ("must go to a DACVB" is stronger than "may be recommended").

**[3] AVSAB 2021 Position Statement on Humane Dog Training. (read in full, 4 pp)**
- Reward-based methods only; "no evidence that aversive methods are more effective ... in any context." Serious problems (aggression, anxiety, fear) need management + behavior modification + "in some cases" medication, and "should always be evaluated by a veterinarian". Medication is not a "last resort". Explicitly concedes that survey evidence "cannot differentiate between causation and correlation". Hiby 2004: reward-only > mixed ("balanced") > aversive-only obedience. Cortisol "can be difficult to interpret" (rises with positive arousal too).
- Supports R-TRI-02, R-REF-09, R-EQ-01, R-MED-01. Note: "no evidence" is an absence claim; see Corrections (Johnson & Wynne RCT).

**[4] AVSAB 2008 Puppy Socialization. (read in full, 2 pp)**
- First 3 months = primary window; socialization before full vaccination should be the standard of care; classes from 7-8 weeks with >=1 vaccine set >=7 days before and a first deworming; keep vaccinations current during class; **avoid dog parks/untrusted areas**; puppies showing fear "should seek veterinary guidance"; positive reinforcement only; alone-time and crate/pen time to prevent over-attachment. Claims "behavioral issues, not infectious disease, are the number one cause of death for dogs under three" (uncited in the text).
- Supports README vaccine-gating and "dog parks excluded".

**[5] AVSAB Position Statement on Dominance Theory. PDF footer: (c) 2008. (read in full, 4 pp)**
- Dominance = relationship established by force/aggression and submission; most owner-unwanted behavior is accidentally rewarded, not rank-seeking; punishment can suppress aggression without treating the cause and raises fear; do not refer to dominance-based trainers. Cites Herron 2008 (conference paper) for confrontational techniques eliciting aggression.
- Supports R-REF-09, R-TRI-02.

**[6] AVSAB Position Statement on Positive Veterinary Care. PDF footer: (c) 2016. (read in full, 4 pp)**
- "It only takes one experience to trigger these responses, and lead to generalized fear." "Aggression is almost always caused by fear." 38% of dog owners say their dog "hates" the vet. Happy visits, counter-conditioning, minimal restraint, basket muzzles paired with food, premedication "as necessary".
- Supports R-VET-01, R-REF-12 (as vet-led).

**[7] AVSAB 2021 Glossary. (read in full, 2 pp).** Definitions: flooding "should be avoided ... risk of invoking a fear response"; systematic desensitization "slow enough not to provoke an appreciable fear response"; counter-conditioning; antecedent arrangement; aversive list (alpha rolls, leash corrections, shaker cans, spray bottles, prong/shock). Source for the data dictionary only.

**[8] AVSAB 2026 How to Choose a Trainer. (read in full, 3 pp).** "If fear, anxiety or aggression are occurring, contact a veterinarian with advanced behavior knowledge, a vet behaviorist (DACVB), or a **credentialed behavior consultant**" (so a credentialed consultant is acceptable, not DACVB-only). Red flags: prong/shock, "alpha/pack leader/dominant", "balanced". Credentials "do not guarantee quality". Supports R-REF-03 (referral target list).

**[9] AVSAB Cesar Millan response. PDF footer (c) 2016; no event date in text. (read in full, 1 p).** Short opinion; punishment methods "likely to exacerbate fear and increase aggression in the long run"; deliberately provoking the target animal puts it at risk. No data. Context only.

**[11] ACVB Board letter to AVMA President, 2 Dec 2025. (read in full, 5 pp)**
- Position letter, not a study. Claims "meta-analyses and primary research" but the reference list has two narrative reviews (Ziv 2017; Fernandes 2017) and primary studies, no meta-analysis. For behavior "serious enough to consider euthanasia" the standard of care is involving a vet with advanced behavior expertise, "ideally a DACVB"; for "potential human injury, public safety, or risk of the animal harming itself" recommend assessment by a board-certified veterinary behaviorist; "early referral because outcomes are better" (no data given). Cites Cooper 2014 as "most rigorous study on electronic collars to date" (recall not better than reward-based) and Johnson & Wynne 2024 (Animals 14(18):2632, chasing study) as showing harms.
- Supports R-REF-10 (euthanasia), R-REF-09. Does **not** mention rehoming.

**[12] ABTC 2022 (March) Information for Veterinary Professionals. (read in full, 4 pp)**
- CAB/VB/AAB "will only see clients on veterinary referral" (so direct-to-behaviourist routes are not available in the UK; client contacting directly is told to see their vet first). Full medical history shared; clinical exam "preferable and strongly recommended" but "may not always be practical". Non-vet behaviourists "must not discuss the use of specific medications with a client"; vet remains prescriber. Post-nominals ABTC-AT/ATI/ABT/AAB/CAB/VB. CAB and CCAB equal standard.
- Supports R-MED-01 (engine must not name drugs), credential-mapping gap (NOTES 6).

**[13] University of Edinburgh. Guidance on providing behavioural advice prior to referral. PDF footer is OCR-garbled and appears to read "Last updated ... 2022" (LINKS says 2025; URL path 2025-04 is only the upload date). (read in full, 4 pp)**
- Six steps: (1) list behaviors and contexts/triggers (example: growl when owner moves within 1 m of food bowl); (2) manage - avoid/minimise triggers, contain (doors, gates, leads, muzzle "if it is safe to do so"), **avoid reinforcing** (distracting with treats "can end up reinforcing the problem behaviour", prefer walking away quietly); (3) rule out obvious medical problems - history, other signs, video of gait/posture; "not necessary to provide a 'clean bill of health' prior to referral"; cites Mills 2020 for "28-82% ... physical health factors"; (4) owner log/diary + safe video; (5) report adjunctive treatments; (6) delay neutering decisions made for behavior. Referral waits are "weeks to months".
- Supports R-MGT-01, R-TRI-05, "interim safety content during suppression" (NOTES 5 gap 4). Qualifies: "treat-distraction" can reinforce, which matters for LAT/engage-disengage and AAHA's "distraction and redirection".

**[14] Dogs Trust / BVBA. Framework for Behavioural Consultation (web page). (read in full)**
- 31% and 35% of owners reported a problem at 6 and 9 months (Lord 2020, Generation Pup). Pain "in 28% to 82% of cases" (Mills 2020; the page calls it a review of 100 cases). Pain-sign table adapted from Hellyer 2007; HCPI/CBPI questionnaires. Medical/drug mechanisms: Cushing's -> eating/drinking -> resource guarding; osteoarthritis -> less able to retreat -> overt fear; CCD -> night barking; steroids polyuria/polydipsia/polyphagia -> guarding; trilostane unmasking arthritis; over-treated hypothyroidism -> excitability. Routine bloods + urinalysis; **analgesia trial "at least four to six weeks, and ideally eight weeks"** alongside behavior modification (no citation given for the duration). RCVS referral criteria quoted (no obvious medical cause / risk of injury to owner or public / complex / long-term management). Behavioural euthanasia "should be considered if" owner cannot care safely, welfare unlikely to improve in reasonable time, or rehoming would compromise welfare; rehoming "could be a kind and sensible option".
- Does NOT list "severity out of proportion", "relapse", "new onset in middle-aged/older dogs" as medical-suspicion clues (NOTES 1.2 attributes them to [14]).
- Supports R-REF-01 (medical rows), R-REF-03. Qualifies R-REF-10 (rehoming is presented as an option, not a referral trigger).

**[15] Dogs Trust Behavioural referrals page. (read in full).** Same RCVS criteria. "Training alone is unlikely to change things because ... the dog also needs to learn to feel differently." "Anybody may call themselves a behaviourist." Registers named: ABTC and CCAB Certification Ltd (the framework page names ABTC and ASAB). Owner must be given first-aid information while arranging referral. CAB/CCAB/VB see only referred cases; only vets prescribe.

**[16] Dogs Trust behavioural first aid (hub page).** Membership/sign-up text only. No evidence content.

**[17] Cornell Riney Canine Health Center - Basket muzzle training (Cohen, Anderson; updated 23 Dec 2025). (read in full)**
- "**All dogs** can benefit from learning to wear a muzzle"; puppyhood ideal. Basket muzzle (Baskerville Ultra, Jafco clear vinyl); constricting nylon "not appropriate for most situations". Sessions 5-10 min; the first stage is stopped "after 1-2 minutes"; practise "a few times a week" until several positive experiences; two stages (nose-in, then strap attached loosely; 1-2 fingers under strap). Do not leave on unattended; do not put the dog in scary situations while still learning. History of handling sensitivity or biting -> consider a positive-reinforcement trainer or DACVB. Bring the muzzle and put it on before entering the clinic if the pet is fearful/aggressive.
- Supports muzzle-conditioning module parameters. Contradicts the blanket "muzzle only after professional input" (R-MGT-05; README REFER_BEHAVIORIST row) for dogs with no bite/handling-sensitivity history.

**[19] Dunbar Academy handout "Putting dog bites into perspective with the Bite Scale". (read in full, 1 p)**
- Levels: 1 no contact; 2 no puncture; 3 shallow puncture; 4 deep puncture; 5 multiple bites; 6 victim dead. Hospital data: 92.4% of "bites" ISS 0, 7.5% ISS 1, <1% ISS >=2, hence "over 99% of dog bites are Level 1 or 2 ... these dogs are not dangerous". Prognosis: L1-2 "wonderful" (basic training, classical conditioning, desensitization, handling, bite-inhibition); L3 "a little dangerous", "fair to good if you have owner compliance"; L4 "danger", work only in "exceptional circumstances with 100% owner compliance and a detailed management protocol", "should not remain in a home with children", prognosis poor; L5-6 "extremely dangerous ... Euthanasia should be considered", "dire".
- Not in this file: "1-4 punctures" for L3 and "multiple L4 bites" for L5 (NOTES 1.6 states them; they match Dunbar's full published scale but are not in the local copy). No cohort data for prognosis; the ISS statistics support injury severity, not prognosis. Rates injury, not context or intent.

**[20] DogStarDaily "The Bite Scale" (Dunbar, post dated 30 Aug 2016; byline "James Lu Dunbar"). (read in full)**
- Narrative only; does not define the six levels itself. "Almost all 'dog bites' are Level 1 or 2." L1-2 "need training right away"; "rehabilitative classical conditioning and hand-feeding is desperately needed immediately." L3+: puncture means bite inhibition "is not reliable"; "You need help from a **professional dog trainer**, and you need it immediately", plus manage so the dog cannot bite again. Note this routes L3+ to a trainer, opposite to AAHA/ACVB.

### Primary studies and reviews

**[22] Feuerbacher EN, Muir CE. 2020. Animals 10(7):1110. (main text read)**
- Single-case experiment, **n=5** dogs recruited from the community (plus one excluded), owner-reported SRPB confirmed by webcam baseline (>=30 min absence). Four treatment sessions of 30-60 min each (not more than 1 week apart) with an experimenter monitoring by webcam; owner return (1 min of normal attention) as reinforcer for DRO (no problem behavior) then DRA (sit/lie down).
- **Step ladder (the only empirical absence ladder in this set):** 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210 s. Advance after **2** successful trials; any problem behavior drops to the last successful criterion, which must be re-earned twice; a failure at the easier level drops one further.
- Results: highest criterion reached > baseline latency (Wilcoxon W=15, p<0.05, one-tailed). Harrison (destructive) reached DRA 300 s; Scarlet similar (94.3-96.1% successful trials); Mei, Sadie, Tristan (vocalizers) stayed at 5-30 s, often reset on trial 1 of each session. Only 1/5 dogs reached 5 min. Treatment felt "unwieldy, demanding, tedious"; intervening absences between sessions hurt progress.
- Limitations: n=5, experimenter-delivered, short follow-up, no generalization test, authors argue greeting/attention on return seems fine and "ignoring may be without merit".
- Supports: R-SEP-01/02 (slow progress; avoid uncontrolled absences between sessions). Contradicts the README/NOTES ladder values (5,10,20,30,60 s, double after 3 trials, back 2) as evidence-based; the study used 2 successes and back-1.

**[24] Dale FC, Burn CC, Murray J, Casey R. 2024. Anim Welf 33:e60. DOI 10.1017/awf.2024.56. (main text read)**
- Exploratory longitudinal cohort (Generation Pup), owner questionnaires. **n=145** analysed from 1,987 respondents (1,218 excluded for missing data, 462 not fitting case/control definition). 46.9% (68/145) met the SRB case definition at 6 months; the definition includes behaviors on return, most common were pacing (14.5%), whining (7.6%), spinning (6.9%).
- Multivariable odds ratios (95% CI): enclosed overnight at <=16 wk OR 0.029 (0.00-0.24); 9 h sleep OR 0.142 (0.03-0.72), 10+ h OR 0.019 (0.00-0.16) vs 6-8 h; >=4 house-training accidents/wk OR 13.25 (2.49-70.4); training reward "kibble other than normal kibble and/or dog food treats" OR 13.85 (2.81-68.3); owners 16-34 y OR 11.1 (1.5-81.7); >=2 aversive techniques when dog "relaxing" OR 27.96 (3.0-260); **time-out methods OR 97.96 (8.4-1148)**; "fussing" (greet/stroke/cuddle/play) on return after bad behavior OR 5.76 (1.24-26.7) vs other responses (abstract says "six times"). Breed, sex, source non-significant.
- Limitations: tiny n, sparse cells (very wide CIs), many tested variables (authors: possible Type I/II errors), owner report, case definition conflates separation and return behavior, correlational. Authors themselves say interventional confirmation is needed.
- Supports R-SEP-01 only weakly. Sleep >=9 h, enclosed overnight space and "no fussing" are **low-confidence**, not "med".

**[25] Almquist E, Meyer I, Sandoe P, Thomassen KM, Newberry RC, Rehn T. 2026. Sci Rep 16:2627. DOI 10.1038/s41598-026-36791-w. (main text read)**
- Qualitative reflexive thematic analysis, **15** semi-structured interviews (5 each Sweden/Denmark/Norway; 4 vets, 11 behaviourists, 9 trainers, overlapping). Six themes: co-occurring behavioural difficulties, a body in flux (adolescence, illness), dog training gone wrong, breed type, routine changes, owner-dog psychological interplay. Authors: themes are "professional observations ... rather than empirically established risk factors". Context numbers: SRP prevalence 4-34% across surveys; Lord et al. 5-8% at 6-9 months; professionals report onset around 6-18 months.
- Use: hypothesis generation only. Supports "adolescent onset needs a vet/behavior screen" as a low-weight rule.

**[38] Baslington-Davies A, Howell H, Hogue TE, Mills DS. 2023. Animals 13(14):2329. (main text read)**
- "Systematic review" with the authors' own relaxed "best use of available evidence" appraisal: 30,233 records -> 704 -> **14 papers** (11 on dogs >12 months; 3 adults). Outcome definitions of human-directed aggression vary widely (some include growling, others only bites); control groups differ; no study examined pain/health or child-specific interactions.
- Selected effect sizes (single studies, heterogeneous): pet-shop source OR 2.4 vs breeder; homed <=7 wk vs later OR 7.08 (one study) but another found no association; non-domestic maternal environment OR 17.86 (5.97-53.4) for stranger aggression away from home only; owner with 5+ prior puppies OR 0.29 (0.16-0.44); table feeding OR 5.86 (1.83-20.8) and 3 (1-8); positive reinforcement only OR 0.2 (0.04-0.95); positive reinforcement + inconsistent punishment OR 1.85 (0.79-4.34, n.s.); puppy class attendance: no relationship with owner-directed aggression.
- Supports: R-REF-03 context, "inexperienced owner" and "source" as intake risk modifiers (not gates). Do not turn any single OR into an engine parameter.

**[41] Anderson KH, Yao Y, Perry PJ, Albright JD, Houpt KA. 2022. Animals 12(5):576. (read in full)**
- Retrospective, single academic referral clinic (Cornell), 1997-2017: **n=1,923** records, principal problem known in 1,800. Aggression 1,300 (**72.2%**; human-directed 79.7% of these, inter-dog 19.0%), anxiety/fear/phobia 362 (20.1%; separation anxiety 49.2% of these, generalized 40.6%, noise 10.2%), miscellaneous 133 (7.4%), normal 5. Breeds 2007-2016: behavior n=822 vs 51,052 hospital patients; Herding (11.2% vs 8.7%) and Terrier (10.6% vs 6.7%) over-represented, mixed breed 38.3% vs 25.6%; Pit-bull-type breeds identification unreliable. Male intact associated with aggression (p=0.0026). Secondary problems not available.
- Limitations (authors): referral bias (owners more motivated to seek help for aggression and large dogs; cost), terminology inconsistent.
- Supports: referral caseloads are aggression-heavy; not a prevalence estimate for pet dogs. R-TRI-04 (aggression label is not a diagnosis).

**[48]-[49] Hooper J, Buchanan-Smith HM, Robertson T, Lambert P. 2025. Animals 15(13):1971. (main text read)**
- Ecological analysis of Scottish NHS 24 calls, A&E attendances and hospital admissions 2007-2019: 59,111 records, 48,599 individuals; 81.6% of A&E dog records found only via free text. Deprived-area rates "at least 2-3 times higher" (decile 1 vs 10; peak for children and adults <70, not >=70). Children are the group with the largest gap in NHS 24/A&E data. Discusses breed-focused law as weakly evidenced and stresses supervision of child-dog contact.
- Limitations: area-level deprivation (ecological fallacy for an individual dog), no dog-level data, case identification by text.
- Use: supports "supervise or separate" content for households with children; do **not** use deprivation as an input.

**[51] Giraudet CSE, Liu K, McElligott AG, Cobb M. 2022. PeerJ 10:e14532. (main text read)**
- Scoping review of **393** documents (SCOPUS, 1980-April 2022; 88% articles). Children are the most at-risk bite group; the highest bite rates range across 0-4, 5-9, 6-12, 0-14 years in different studies. Most bites occur when a young child is left alone with a dog; experts recommend **adult supervision or physical separation**. Dogs may show more stress around children; benefits outweigh risks for children "but not for dogs".
- Supports R-REF-05, R-MGT-05. Child age cutoff is not a single number (see rule table).

**[52] Hammond A, Rowland T, Mills DS, Pilot M. 2022. Evol Appl 15(11):1806-1819. (main text read)**
- Online owner-report survey (UK/Ireland, social-media recruited, self-selected): DIAS and PANAS compared between **8 legislated breeds/types and 17 non-legislated breeds**. Legislated vs not: aggression-threshold factor mean difference 0.00 (-0.02, 0.01); overall DIAS 0.01 (0.00-0.03); all effect sizes small; large within-breed variation. Cites Morrill 2022: breed explains "only 9% of behavioural variation", heritability h2 >0.25 for most traits; Schalke 2008: legislated breeds behaved appropriately in 95% of tests.
- Limitations (authors): owner bias, self-selection; DIAS/PANAS are impulsivity/affect scales, not bite records.
- Supports: breed weighting 0.25 prior (convention; 9% variance is from Morrill via Hammond, not tested here).

**[35] Hakanen E, Mikkola S, Salonen M, Puurunen J, Sulkama S, Araujo C, Lohi H. 2020. Sci Rep 10:13774. (main text read)**
- Online owner survey of **13,700** Finnish dogs (same dataset as Salonen [34]); analysed subsets: fireworks fear n=9,613 (2,881 fearful), thunder 9,513, novel situations 6,945, surfaces/heights 2,932. Logistic regression: less socialization (chi2 26.2, p<0.0001 fireworks); only dog in household OR 1.44 / 1.45 (fireworks/thunder); first-time owner OR 1.53; seldom/never training/activities vs weekly OR 1.41 (fireworks), 1.84 (novel situations); neutered vs intact OR ~1.3-1.5 (thunder 1.55); >3 h exercise was *more* fear-associated than 1-3 h (OR 0.83/0.78); small dogs more fearful; probability rises to ~10 y (fireworks) then falls. Noise sensitivity 32%, novel-situation fear 11%, surfaces/heights 24% (cited from Salonen).
- Limitations: cross-sectional, social-media owner report, causation unclear (fearful dogs may be trained/exercised less).
- Supports intake modifiers; no gate.

**[44] Honeckman L. 2026. Case Rep Vet Med 2026:9804544. (main text read)**
- n=1, 9-month Australian Shepherd with growling from 9 weeks, multiple bites (one scored Dunbar L3, the overall pattern "Level 5" by the author), litter removed from dam at 6 wk, minimal socialization, 6-wk giardiasis. Medical work-up (CBC, chemistry, bile acids, neuro/ophthalmic) normal. Prior advice from breeder/trainers/vet minimised it; rehoming/behavioral euthanasia was under discussion. Plan: leash/harness at home, gates/x-pens, triggers log, **no head collar and no basket muzzle initially** (head/face handling aggression), stop all corrections, Overall relaxation protocol, biweekly in-home sessions with certified positive trainer for 16 wk, fluoxetine 0.6 mg/kg (below range) + gabapentin 13 mg/kg then raised. 6 wk ~25% subjective reduction; 14 wk ~75% subjective; bite behavior resolved after 1 year. Subjective, no C-BARQ.
- Supports: R-REF-10 (rehoming/euthanasia talk), R-MGT-05, "early referral". Qualifies muzzle/head-halter advice for head-handling aggression. Illustrates inconsistent self-grading of the Dunbar scale.

**[57] Blackwell EJ, Bolster C, Richards G, Loftus BA, Casey RA. 2012. BMC Vet Res 8:93. (main text read)**
- Convenience owner survey (England): remote e-collar use 3.3% (n=133), bark e-collar 1.4% (54), electronic fence 0.9% (36). Model comparing e-collar with matched other-method groups explains ~10% of variance (gender, agility, puppy classes). Higher proportion of reward-group owners reported training success than e-collar owners (owner report).
- Context only.

**[56] China L, Mills DS, Cooper JJ. 2020. Front Vet Sci 7:508. (main text read)** and **[55] Cooper JJ, Cracknell N, Hardiman J, Wright H, Mills D. 2014. PLoS One 9(9):e102722. (main text read)**
- Both report the **same trial**: 63 pet dogs referred for recall/off-lead problems, 3 groups of 21 (ECMA-nominated trainers with e-collar; same trainers without; APDT-member trainers, reward-based), 150 min over 5 days (Cooper: two 15-min sessions/day for 4-5 days). Allocation matched on owner-reported problem severity (Cooper) / "randomly allocated" (China, for the 2010 groups). Two trainers delivered Groups A/B.
- Cooper: Group A more time tense, more yawning, less environmental interaction than Group C; **no significant difference in urinary corticosteroids or salivary cortisol** (Group C higher than B); 92% of owners reported improvement, no efficacy difference by owner report; e-collar owners less confident applying the method; a preliminary n=9 phase with high-intensity stimuli without warning showed negative behavior change and raised cortisol.
- China: reward-based group had better single-command Sit/Come responses and shorter latency; **no significant difference in proportion of commands disobeyed** among the three groups.
- Limitations: n=21/group, trainer confound, industry trainers using low settings with warning tones.
- These are one study, not two independent controlled studies.

**[59] DeLeeuw JL, Williams TJ. 2026. Front Vet Sci 13:1744448. (main text read)**
- Qualitative interviews + pre-screen survey, **n=35** US trainers from CCPDT/IAABC/IACP (17 reward-based, 18 mixed). All say positive reinforcement is most used and effective; mixed-method trainers rated positive punishment **as effective as** positive reinforcement; certification type strongly associated with approach (chi2(2,35)=15.2, p<0.001, V=0.66). Industry unregulated. Its literature section calls the **only RCT** (Johnson & Wynne 2024, Animals 14:2632, e-collar vs food for stopping lure chasing) an exception: only e-collar dogs ceased the behavior within 5 days (and only they yelped), limited by small n, an extra training session for that group, and an unvalidated food reinforcer.
- Use: supports "credentials do not guarantee methods"; flags an evidence gap (see Corrections).

25. **R-REF-01 "new problem behavior in dog >= 7 y ... N = 4-6 weeks".** AAHA gives size-dependent senior ages (monitoring from 5-8 y large, 8-10 y small); "7 y" and "sudden onset < ~4 wk" have no source. The 4-6 weeks is the analgesia-trial length [14], not a no-progress trigger. Flag both as convention.
26. **R-REF-05 "children (<~12 y)".** Hooper: social gradient strongest for children <=14; Giraudet: highest bite rates 0-4, 5-9, 6-12 or 0-14 depending on study. Use <14 (or <15) or "any child in household" rather than 12.
27. **R-REF-10 "considering rehoming/euthanasia -> REFER_DACVB [11, 14]".** ACVB covers behavior "serious enough to consider euthanasia"; it does not mention rehoming. Dogs Trust calls rehoming "a kind and sensible option" and lists criteria for behavioural euthanasia without prescribing a DACVB. Rehoming-as-trigger is a convention (reasonable, but flag it).
28. **R-REF-02 "any bite with skin contact (Dunbar L>=2)" -> DACVB.** Dunbar calls L1-2 dogs "not dangerous"; AAHA names deep bites and immunocompromised victims. The L2 gate is a conservative convention. NOTES already marks med; keep it flagged.
29. **R-MGT-03 head halter / R-EQ-01 for reactivity.** Honeckman avoided a head collar and basket muzzle where aggression was linked to head/face handling; AAHA says "immediately stop using a head collar if the dog feels trapped or panics". Make head-halter conditional on no head-handling aggression.
30. **README "never recommend medication ... a veterinarian can discuss whether medication is appropriate".** Supported by ABTC (non-vets must not discuss specific drugs). But AAHA calls medication first-line for SRP/noise phobia; the safe engine phrasing should still be neutral, not "adjunct only".
31. **NOTES 1.3 "no evidence [aversives] outperform reward-based methods [2,3,10,11,68]".** The position statements say this, but DeLeeuw and ACVB point to Johnson & Wynne 2024 (RCT, n small) where e-collar dogs stopped chasing in 5 days; AVSAB itself concedes most evidence is correlational. The policy conclusion (no aversives) rests on welfare risk plus parity of outcomes, not on a proven efficacy deficit. Keep the rule; soften the evidential claim.
32. **README intake "highest bite level (Dunbar 1-6)" as an owner field.** Dunbar levels need puncture depth relative to canine length and number of bites; Honeckman's own grading is inconsistent (L3 and "Level 5" for one dog). Ask observable fields (contact? broken skin? needed medical care? number of bites? child involved?) and derive the level.

## 4. Gaps and open questions

1. **No source gives a validated threshold** for: sudden-onset window, senior age cutoff, "no progress" weeks, SRP "mild" duration, DS/CC trial counts and back-step size, session minutes for SRP/noise, children's age cutoff. All are tunable constants; add them with a "convention" tag in the constants file.
2. **The Johnson & Wynne 2024 RCT (Animals 14:2632) is missing** from LINKS and from the evidence summary; read it before asserting "no efficacy deficit". The pulling-equipment paper (PeerJ) is a different study.
3. **Bite-scale definition source.** The local Dunbar copy lacks puncture counts/depths; fetch the full scale (Dunbar academy) or ask the advisor, and design owner-intake questions that avoid self-grading.
4. **Dunbar prognosis has no cohort data.** The ISS statistics are about injury severity in treated incidents.
5. **AAHA vs engine on separation anxiety and mild fear.** AAHA calls SRP an emergency with first-line medication and asks for vet assessment of any distress at social maturity; the engine allows mild-case DIY. Decide the CAUTION banner wording ("mention this to your vet") with the DACVB advisor.
6. **Credential mapping.** ABTC (UK) requires vet referral for CAB/VB/AAB; AVSAB (US) allows a "credentialed behavior consultant". Map by region.
7. **Treat-distraction vs reinforcement.** Edinburgh warns it can reinforce; AAHA lists distraction/redirection as a technique; LAT/engage-disengage evidence is absent. Needs expert guidance before the engine suggests "treat to distract".
8. **Dale's unexplained findings** (training reward type OR 13.85; time-out OR 97.96) are unreplicated, but if anyone reads the paper they will conflict with "small soft food by default" and with time-out advice. Check the supplementary tables before relying on any single Dale variable.
9. **Dataset overlap** among Finnish papers (Salonen/Hakanen/Mikkola/Sulkama/Tiira): resolve to avoid double-counting.
10. **Muzzle policy.** Decide: general muzzle conditioning for all dogs without bite history (Cornell) vs "professional input" for dogs with a bite or head/face sensitivity (Honeckman).
11. **Large journal papers** (Dale, Hooper, Giraudet, DeLeeuw, Baslington-Davies, Hammond) were read as extracted main text but not line by line in the introductions; none of the omitted parts is cited by NOTES, but supplementary tables were not available.
