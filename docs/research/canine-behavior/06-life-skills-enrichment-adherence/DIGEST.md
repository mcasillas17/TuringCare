# DIGEST: 06 Life skills, enrichment, owner adherence (deep-read pass)

Date 2026-11-08. Scope: all 27 files in `sources/`, all 43 rows of `LINKS.md`, `NOTES.md`, `../README.md`. Nothing else in the repo was modified.

**Read-status key.** `FULL` = whole text incl. all body prose read (5 sources). `BODY` = abstract plus all body prose after the Introduction (methods, results, discussion, limitations) read in a second pass (22 sources); Introduction/background paragraphs, reference lists, supplements and numeric tables skimmed or skipped. Rows below that still say `SECTIONS` mean `BODY`; `2nd pass` marks details added by that pass. `ABSTRACT/SECONDARY` = not downloaded; abstract, publisher/repository record or secondary summary only (stated per row). `NOT RETRIEVED` = nothing usable obtained.
Rule ids `A1..H33` = numbered rules in NOTES section 3 (1-33, written `N#`); `README §x` = engine synthesis. `#n` = LINKS row.

**Headline.** (1) The ">5 instructions hurt outcomes" claim traces to one 52-dog clinic cohort (Takeuchi 2000, abstract-level) and was then repeated as a recommendation by three later papers. It is an association, not a trial, and it was about separation-anxiety discharge instructions, not weekly training plans. (2) Session-length minutes (1/3/5) are pure expert guidance (Fear Free handout, no data). (3) The spacing studies measure sessions needed to reach criterion, not calendar time. (4) The sleep/Kis result is narrower than NOTES says: it is the _interfering-learning_ group that failed to consolidate; sleep, walk and play all improved at 1 week. (5) The 60% time-budget factor and the 40/50/80% thresholds have no study behind them, and in the one study that tested time-type barriers (LAIR) they did not predict success. (6) Several citations are wrong (Demant DOI, Sargisson authorship, #4, #43, 64% attribution).

**S1. Daniels JT, Busby D, Chase-Topping M, Brown SM. 2023. "I wish he'd listen": client-centered interviewing... J Vet Behav 63:22-30. doi:10.1016/j.jveb.2023.03.003 (CC BY).** FULL (text extracted with PDFKit; the earlier agent could not read it).

- Design: UK online survey (Facebook convenience sample, Dec 2020-Feb 2021) of adults who paid a trainer/behaviourist/vet for a dog behaviour problem in the last 2 y. n=230 analysed (238 minus non-UK/missing): 142 trainer, 84 behaviourist, 4 vet clients; 87.4% female; dogs needing medication/supplements excluded. 30 Likert items, PCA, Spearman, Mann-Whitney, thematic analysis of a free-text question (n=227).
- Results: one significant PC (P<0.001) explained 57.1% of variance. Positive pole = "plan was right for my dog", "professional supportive", "agreed on plan"; negative pole = "felt judged", "plan included measures I disagreed with". PC1 correlated with self-reported compliance: full r=0.567, partial r=0.373 (both P<0.001). Face-to-face vs remote and trainer vs behaviourist not significant (P=0.569, 0.275). Free-text themes (percent of responses, can exceed 100): agreeing with methods 26.9% (61), achieving results 24.2%, following up 13.7%, welfare concern 13.2%, tailoring plan 10.6%, understanding plan 7.9%, understanding behaviour 7.5%, finding time only 2.0% (6). Incremental/simple plan quote: "Simple, clear steps with increases in difficulty as improvement noticed".
- Limitations (authors): self-reported compliance, over-reporting; improvement not quantified; self-selected Facebook sample; compliance duration unknown; confirmation bias. Philosophical match on methods ("balanced" vs force-free) strongly shapes adherence.
- Instructions: states "five or fewer instructions have been recommended to maximize BMP adherence for separation-related problems (Sargisson, 2014)" and "clients need simple BMP instructions". It does not measure instruction count.
- Engine: supports co-creation/choice (N10), "why each exercise exists" (N10), small incremental steps (N11, N12), follow-up check-ins (add), flexible tailored plan (N14). Contradicts nothing. Caution: the free-text table gives "incrementing" n=16 as 26.9% (a typo; 16/227 = 7%), so the table cannot be quoted for that theme.

**S2. Fear Free Happy Homes. "Training Session Length" handout (hosted at avsab.org, 2022).** FULL (1 page).

- Quotes: "three minutes is plenty long for most dogs. Dogs new to training should be given even shorter sessions. Puppies might only be able to train for 1 minute"; "Many dogs (especially experienced dogs) are capable of training for longer, but three minutes is a good guideline"; timer "2 to 3 minutes" or "pre-count... 10 to 20 treats"; "When in doubt, shorter is better!"; sessions can be "just one repetition long"; end with treats/chew/game/walk; cats half the time.
- No citations, no data. Pure practitioner guidance.
- Engine: supports A2 values for puppy/new/adult (3 min) as convention. Does NOT support "experienced adult up to 5 min" nor "hard max 5 min" (the handout says experienced dogs _can_ train longer but recommends 3; 5 is invented). Supports A6/session ending ritual and N13 "shorter is better" tip.

**S3. Kis A, Szakadat S, Gacsi M, et al. 2017. The interrelated effect of sleep and learning in dogs. Sci Rep 7:41873. doi:10.1038/srep41873.** SECTIONS (all results and methods).

- Exp.1: n=15 adult pet dogs (mean 3.67 y), 3 h polysomnography after a command-learning task (unfamiliar words for sit/down) vs a non-learning task. Learning raised relative NREM delta (t(14)=2.94, p=0.011), lowered alpha. Performance rose after 3 h sleep (t(14)=3.83, p=0.002; 18-trial test) but improvement was not related to sleep duration; no awake control in Exp.1.
- Exp.2: n=53 adult pet dogs, learned the task, 18-trial baseline, then a 1 h retention interval (quasi-random): sleep (n=14, in owner's parked car), on-leash walk (14), learning an unrelated task with owner in 10-min sessions (12), playing with a stuffed Kong (13); retest right after and ~7.6 d later. Mean % correct (baseline/retest/long-term): Sleep 57.5/59.5/67.8; Walk 49.2/54.4/61.1; Learn 55.9/51.9/56.5; Play 49.0/43.9/63.1.
- Interpretation by authors: no significant immediate gain for any group (Play dropped at retest, p<0.001, attention/arousal). At 1 week Sleep, Walk and Play all beat baseline (p<0.001); the Learn group did not improve (baseline vs long-term p>0.05): "an interfering learning experience impedes memory consolidation". Consolidation attributed to normal night sleep at home.
- Limitations: small cells (12-14), quasi-random allocation, tasks = re-labelling known actions, 1 h interval only, Nestle-Purina funded, no group had no-retention-interval control. 2nd pass: whole paper read; no further change.
- Engine: supports only "do not schedule new unrelated learning in the hour after a lesson" (A6) at low-med confidence. Contradicts NOTES/LINKS wording "dogs that slept or walked did better a week later; those that played did not gain at the immediate retest": play also improved at 1 week; the sleep-vs-walk-vs-play difference is not established. There is no evidence for a "5-10 min wind-down sleep block" (A6 is a convention).

**S4. China L, Mills DS, Cooper JJ. 2020. Efficacy of dog training with and without remote electronic collars vs a focus on positive reinforcement. Front Vet Sci 7:508.** SECTIONS (abstract, methods, results, discussion).

- Design: 63 dogs (>9 months, referred for poor recall/livestock worrying, no e-collar history), 3 groups of 21: E-collar (manufacturer-nominated trainers), Control 1 (same trainers, no electric stimuli), Control 2 (APDT UK trainers, positive reinforcement). "up to 150 min of training over 5 days"; analysed video from "the two training sessions on the first, third and fifth day". All dogs wore a (dummy) collar for blinding. Dogs were "primarily kept on 10 m long leads"; ~70% of time within 1 m of trainer, <5% >5 m away in all groups. Outcomes: commands needed, response latency for Come and Sit.
- Results: 20.3 commands/session; 77% obeyed first command. Control 2 gave fewer commands, fewer hand/lead signals, shorter latency than E-collar for both Come and Sit; mean Come latency 1.24 s; proportion disobeyed did not differ between groups. Food used ~5x more often in Control 2 (previous work).
- Limitations: Control 2 trained 4 months later (spring 2011 vs midwinter), matched not randomised, different trainers, task selection differed (C2 mostly recall), lab-field mix with penned sheep/chickens; outcome = in-session response, no retention or real-world recall. 2nd pass: authors say differences "may reflect general differences in training style of the trainer groups... rather than use of E-collar per se"; Control 1 (same trainers, no e-collar) did no better than the e-collar group; C2 used mainly one verbal cue + one food contingency, no lead pressure; shock vs vibration events could not be identified from video; DEFRA funded.
- Engine: supports "no aversive tools" (policy) and fewer/consistent cues (A23). It is not a dosing study and not a distance-recall study (dogs mostly within 1 m).

**S5. Vieira de Castro AC et al. 2020. Does training method matter? PLoS ONE 15(12):e0225023.** SECTIONS.

- Design: quasi-experimental, 92 companion dogs from 7 schools: Reward n=42, Mixed n=22 (low aversive), Aversive n=28 (high aversive). 3 video sessions, 6 saliva samples, then a spatial cognitive-bias task.
- Results: baseline cortisol equal (0.13-0.15 ug/dL); post-training 0.26 (Aversive) vs 0.23 (Mixed) vs 0.13 (Reward) ug/dL (H(2)=8.63, p=0.013); Aversive and Mixed showed more stress behaviours, tension, panting; Aversive more pessimistic bias.
- Limits: authors state "quasi-experimental rather than experimental... cannot infer true causal relationship"; school self-selection; reviewers flagged over-interpretation of judgement bias; groups differed in owner gender, children at home, dog age, breed group (age/children/gender tested as confounders and reported robust; breed and age of separation not analysable: 34% mixed/unknown breed, 22% unknown separation age).
- Engine: supports "never recommend aversives" as policy with converging, not causal, evidence (README safety constants).

**S6. AVSAB. 2021. Position Statement on Humane Dog Training.** FULL (first ~half and FAQ) / SECTIONS.

- Reward-based methods for all training; "no evidence that aversive training is necessary"; admits survey evidence "cannot differentiate between causation and correlation"; cites Hiby 2004, de Castro 2020, China 2020 (no difference in e-collar vs reward for recall). Lists tools to avoid (prong, choke, shock, squirt bottles, alpha rolls, flooding); "learner must always... opt out". Expert consensus, no new data.
- Engine: policy layer (README §2 constants).

**S7. Sargisson RJ (2014). Canine separation anxiety: strategies for treatment and management. Vet Med Res Rep 5:143-151 (PMC7521022). [LINKS #18 wrongly "Sherman BL, Mills DS".]** SECTIONS (essentially all).

- Narrative review (Waikato). Key statements: DS + counter-conditioning "may be the most successful treatment"; initial absences "a few seconds", return, praise; vocalisation latency 3.25 min, destruction 7.13 min after departure; if behaviour worsens "reduce the length of their absences again"; food before departure; do not use punishment; video-monitor. Crate: dogs in crates "no less likely to display" SRPB than free-access dogs, crating can raise lip licking and injury. Owners should avoid very long absences but also avoid long periods without any absence; "regular absences of short-to-medium lengths".
- Instructions: "advice... limited to no more than five separate instructions [ref 33 = Takeuchi 2000]"; Herron et al.: advice to exercise, avoid punishment, give a food-stuffed toy at departures; "none of which prevented" SRPB; the stuffed toy was the only advice reliably followed and "did not have a significant effect on the incidence". Dog owners are more non-compliant "with instructions that involve a large time commitment" (Takeuchi). Butler: DS worked in 8 dogs even with owner non-compliance.
- Engine: supports A25 method (seconds, back a step on worsening) and crate-not-for-separation (A27). The stuffed-toy item is "most reliably followed" but not shown effective (N18/A18 over-reads it).

**S8. Feuerbacher EN, Muir KL. 2020. Using owner return as a reinforcer to operantly treat SRPB. Animals 10(7) (PMC7401621).** SECTIONS.

- Design: 6 recruited, 5 completed; 30-min baseline webcam absence; four treatment sessions (up to ~60 min) with DRO/DRA, owners coached, criteria raised/lowered per trial by observed problem behaviour.
- Results: problem behaviour fell vs baseline but "after four sessions, only one dog was able to stay alone for at least 5 min". Notes earlier DS trial's 5-min start criterion "was likely too long" and that owner-set pacing (increase rate) confounds prior studies. Relays Takeuchi: "compliance dropped when owners were given more than five instructions", owners followed easy steps (no punishment) but not desensitisation; prior pharmacology trials gave owners 7-12 bullet instructions.
- Engine: supports A25 (start below threshold, performance-driven progression) and shows progress is slow (do not promise outcomes).
- 2nd pass (body read): criterion rule used = advance only after **two successful trials**; **one** problem-behaviour (PB) trial drops back to the last successful criterion (DRO steps +5 s to 15 s, +15 s to 60 s, then +30 s). Baseline mean latency to first PB 27.1 s (all <2 min), so the earlier study's 5-min start was above threshold. When criteria rose too fast PB recurred on the next trial 30% of the time (0-50% by dog); in 14 of 18 consecutive-PB sequences PB came *sooner* the second time (re-sensitisation): supports "retreat a step, do not repeat the failing step". 4 sessions of 30-60 min, <=1 week apart; other cited trials needed 3-9 h of training for 6-30 min alone; "practitioners set expectations of weeks to months"; authors suspect the operant contingency (owner return) may underlie DS effects and note low owner compliance with complex steps. Only dog-specific 2-up/1-down rule in the folder (n=5, untested against alternatives); it does not support the 80/60/40% thresholds (A1).

**S9. Harris L et al. 2019. Impact of socio-economic status on accessibility of dog training classes (Dogs Trust). Animals 9(10):849.** SECTIONS.

- Pilot: free face-to-face 3-class course (week 1 presentation without dogs, 2 practical): 51 started, 29 finished (43% dropout); free online (19 sections): 32 started, none completed (12 opened any section); paid classes same region: 58 started, 44 completed (24%). Completers had higher income (p=0.049). Reasons from only 13 of the dropouts: illness 5, work 4, dog unsuitable 2, season 1, rehomed 1.
- Limits: course completion, not home practice adherence; tiny reasons sample; fee as commitment device confounded with SES. 2nd pass: dog ages differed (online 5 y, face-to-face 2 y, paid classes 5 months) so paid vs free is not like-for-like; authors cite the sunk-cost effect; completers' attitude scores rose (ITDS p=0.004, paid p<0.0001; median +4 both).
- Engine: supports short fixed reason list incl. illness/work (N14) but reasons n=13 is anecdotal; none of it calibrates a completion threshold.

**S10. Generation Pup, puppy training class attendance. 2025 (PMC12427422).** SECTIONS.

- n=2187 UK puppy owners; 67.1% (1468) attended at least one class by 16-wk survey; 90.4% female; odds higher with income, intention, first-time owner, information pack; common non-attendance reasons: wanting to work with the puppy themselves, no suitable class. 37.6% of non-attenders (270/719) intended to attend. Self-selected cohort; selection bias acknowledged.
- 2nd pass: classes varied widely; recall and sit taught in over half, house-training advice absent in 57%; lower attendance with older acquisition age partly vaccination status/class start ages (12-16 wk). Engine: supports DIY-friendly delivery and explicit house-training content; no engine rule depends on the %.

**S11. Kinsman R et al. 2025. Owner-perceived undesirable behaviours in young dogs. Animals 15:1163 (PMC12024356).** SECTIONS.

- Generation Pup, free-text owner reports at 6/9/12/15/18 months. Dogs with >=1 undesirable behaviour: 29.9% [27.7-32.1] (n=1718), 34.0, 42.1 [39.3-44.9] (1219), 35.1, 32.0 [28.6-35.6]. Behaviour prevalence at 12 months: barking 13.4%, jumping 7.4%, pulling on lead 6.6%, recall issues 6.6%, fear 3.9%. At 6 months barking 7.9%, pulling 3.8%, recall 3.3%. All four focal behaviours peaked at 12 months; jumping did not differ significantly across ages. Median 1 behaviour per dog.
- 2nd pass: authors say post-12-month declines may reflect maturation, owner training/acceptance or changed perception (not separable); free-text under-counts; agonistic behaviour rose 0.9% -> 3.4% (6 -> 18 mo); self-selected cohort. Engine: supports H32 pre-warning of a 12-month peak. Contradicts NOTES ("pulling and recall problems top the owner-reported list"): barking and jumping are the top two; pulling/recall are 3rd-5th at 3-7%.

**S12. Dinwoodie IR, Zottola V, Dodman NH. 2021. Pre-adolescent training and canine behavior. Animals 11 (PMC8147152).** SECTIONS.

- Retrospective online survey (Center for Canine Behavior Studies/social media): 641 owners, 1023 dogs (median age 7 y; 99% had >=1 problem; 78% house soiling). 48% attended puppy training. Attendance by 6 months: OR 0.71 aggression (0.53-0.97), 0.64 compulsive, 0.60 destructive, 0.68 barking, and 1.56 house soiling (1.08-2.27). Start age 1-3 vs 4 vs 5-6 months: no difference. Dogs acquired <=12 wk: OR 0.65 fear/anxiety.

Limits: recall bias, no severity, heavily problem-skewed sample.
- Engine: H33 ok as low confidence.

**S13. Kinsman R et al. 2020. Sleep duration and behaviours in dogs to 12 months. Animals 10 (PMC7401528).** SECTIONS.

- Generation Pup owner reports at 16 wk (n=2332) and 12 months (n=1091). Owner estimates of min/max hours (daytime and nighttime undefined by the questionnaire). Dogs with data at both times: night 7.0 h (SD 1.7) vs 7.3 (1.6), n=662; day median 3.5 vs 3.0 h; total 11.2 h (SD 2.9) vs 10.8 (2.7), n=352 (diff 0.3 h, 95% CI 0.1-0.6). 59.4%/53.6% settled "in a room without human company"; of dogs with night access to people, 86.7%/86.6% chose to be near them. "Sleeping place in the last 7 days" (multiple response): kennel/crate 66.6% (1550/2328) at 16 wk, 36.3% at 12 months; dog bed 38.8% -> 49.9%.
- Limits: owners not watching; asleep vs resting confused. Authors say compare cautiously.
- Engine: supports a wide sleep expectation (mean +/- 1 SD = about 8-14 h) (D20). Contradicts "puppies and seniors higher": puppies reported only 0.3-0.4 h more than 12-month dogs; no senior data in this file. "Crate use 67% -> 36%" is actually sleeping place, not management crating.

**S14. Owczarczak-Garstecka SC, Burman OHP. 2016. Sleep and resting as indicators of welfare in shelter dogs. PLoS ONE 11:e0163620 (PMC5061428).** SECTIONS.

- 15 shelter dogs, CCTV, five non-consecutive days. 660 min asleep per 24 h (44.9%); 71.8% of night, only 2.8% of day asleep; 32 night sleep bouts. More daytime _resting_ associated with positive judgement bias, less repetitive behaviour, more "relaxed" staff codes. Authors: cannot show causation; resting could reflect apathy or learned helplessness (cf. unenriched mink).
- Engine: D20 "protect quiet rest blocks" is plausible but this is a shelter, n=15; do not export numbers to home dogs. 2nd pass: 20 recruited (Battersea), 17 judgement-bias test, 15 video, 12 with all variables (the key regression n=12, R2=0.80: latency to ambiguous probe and repetitive behaviour predicted daytime resting); 1,800 observed hours; intra-rater agreement 92%; reduced daytime sleep may be compensated at night.

**S15. Schipper LL, Vinke CM, Schilder MBH, Spruijt BM. 2008. The effect of feeding enrichment toys on the behaviour of kennelled dogs. Appl Anim Behav Sci 114:182-195.** SECTIONS.

- Pilot in laboratory kennels: Toy group n=8 vs sham control n=9; 20-min video per dog/day across pre-toy, toy (Kong Extreme stuffed with treats), post-toy trials. More appetitive behaviour, more activity/transitions, lower barking frequency; "at least in the short-term"; habituation not seen in the short period.
- Engine: weak support for chew/lick items (D18) for kennelled/laboratory dogs only; no pet-home or alone-time data.

**S16. Chan, Arellano, Horowitz. 2023. Short-term provision of toys does not improve welfare in companion dogs. Animals (PMC10648485).** SECTIONS.

- Companion dogs, 12 days of toys (more complex vs less complex vs control socialising with food), 32 experimental + 15 control enrolled, 7 did not finish. No significant change in activity, rate of food consumption or cognitive bias; slight (non-significant) improvement in bias with complex toys.
- Engine: toys alone are not a welfare fix; also contradicts using it to _prefer_ chews over novelty toys (the study says nothing on chews vs toys). 2nd pass: authors report a **toy-assignment error** (some "lower-level" dogs may have received toys simpler than usual), so complex-vs-simple contrasts are unreliable; every comparison non-significant (approach latency p=0.905 vs lower-level, 0.373 vs control; eating p=0.318/0.519; activity p=0.592/0.614); all dogs became less active after the period; age/sex/breed and lifestyle did not correlate; repeated ambiguous trials create learning effects.

**S17. Enrichment pilot, assistance dogs-in-training. 2022 Animals (PMC8772568).** SECTIONS.

- n=10 neutered dogs, 12-14 months, office-housed; 7 activities (bonding, bubbles, conspecific play, interactive food toy, playhouse, stuffed food toy, tug) each twice over 8 weeks, pre/post behaviour. Enrichment overall raised relaxation (p<0.01), lowered alert (p<0.01) and stress (p=0.02). Conspecific play and playhouse largest change; the two food-based items "had the least behaviour change". Authors: pilot, single assessor, no inter-rater check.
- Engine: contradicts using #31 as support for D18 "chew/lick over novelty"; supports mixing enrichment types. 2nd pass: food items removed once empty (15-min post window), dogs not permanently kennelled (authors' explanation for small food effect); recommend rotating novel activities; social contact (conspecific play) largest effect, consistent with Wells & Hepper.

**S18. Lincoln Adherence Instrument Record (LAIR). Front Vet Sci 2018;5:37 (PMC5845580).** SECTIONS.

- 42 clients of one university behaviour clinic (86 selected, 54 responded, 12 anonymous excluded), outcome = client-rated treatment success (5-pt). Clinicians overestimated success by 0.5 pt. 11 of 29 items correlated with success (Spearman): lack of trust in clinician rho -0.55; distress to pet over time -0.53; complexity of the plan -0.48; too much information -0.45; plan included measures tried before -0.41; also terminology, too little info, immediate distress, "not following all advice to an appropriate standard", no family support. Ordinal regression kept 3 items: lack of trust OR 2.09 (1.02-4.31); previously-tried recommendations OR 2.64 (1.39-5.02); distress to the animal over time OR 8.05 (2.39-27.11).
- Statements on rates: "AAHA (7) study of veterinary medicine reported an overall adherence of 64%, reducing to 20-30% for certain treatments"; human medicine (Martin et al.) "average adherence... 60%, but where treatment is complex or requires lifestyle changes, this falls to 30%".
- Time-type items (time, physical resources, change, clinic support) "did not correlate with treatment success"; the cluster of items about integrating treatment into owner's life predicted nothing in this clinic.
- Limits: n=42, one clinic, convenience, cross-sectional. 2nd pass (discussion): authors caution that non-predictive factors (time, resources) "does not mean that they are not important in general" (uniformly high adherence leaves no variance to predict); all clients disagreed there was a problem understanding the clinician; "distress over time" clustered with understanding of the consultation, and "previous negative experience" with perceived immediate distress and creation of other problems; authors recommend explaining the rationale in plain language.
- Engine: supports owner-distress/trust as top adherence levers and plan simplicity (N11). Does not show time is unimportant, only that it did not discriminate in this clinic.

**S19. Caregiver reports of child-family dog interactions. Front Vet Sci 2017;4:130 (Arhant C et al.).** SECTIONS.

- Self-selected German-language online questionnaire, N=402 caregivers of a child <=6 y and a family dog; benign child behaviours most frequent (mean 4.1/6, rises with age), resource-related 2.1; dog allowing child to take objects from mouth 4.1; supervision-attitude vs child behaviour (allow unsafe behaviours vs benign behaviour rs=0.47); 12% of dogs frequently withdrew from child. "Bites mostly at home by familiar dog" is background literature cited in the intro, not a finding.
- Engine: supports caregiver-directed child-dog content (C30). 2nd pass: 11 bite incidents in total (~3% of pairs, all minor; about one fifth of dogs caused minor injuries, mostly paws/knocking over); commonest bite circumstance = a **resting dog** disturbed by the child (authors: preventable by an inaccessible resting place); resource-related injuries occurred while feeding treats or playing fetch; **no** incidents reported during benign interactions; frontal approach to a stationary dog elicited more growling than passing; dogs that lived in the family before the child showed more fear; forcing contact on a fearful dog may sensitise it; growls should not be punished; spatial/temporal separation advised. Supports C30 (retreat space, no forced contact, no punishing growls); "bites often during benign contact" in NOTES is background literature, not this survey's finding. Correlational; "drive" in NOTES is too causal.

**S20. Owner personality/attachment and treatment outcomes. Front Vet Sci 2020 (131 dyads).** SECTIONS.

- C-BARQ at baseline, 3, 6 months after a vet-behaviour service visit; owner TIPI and LAPS at baseline. Trainability up (coef 0.03), chasing/separation behaviour/energy down. Baseline problem severity was "the most consistent predictor" (e.g. non-social fear OR 6.58, owner-directed aggression OR 6.76 for decrease at 6 months; probably regression to the mean). Owner conscientiousness, extraversion, openness, attachment associated with some categories; conscientiousness had 53% _lower_ odds of a decrease in stranger-directed aggression at 3 months.
- Engine: NOTES "Owner traits... predict 3-6 month outcomes" over-states; effects are mixed and inconsistent. Not a basis for any rule. 2nd pass: extraversion/openness effects did not persist to 6 months; attachment effects "relatively weak and inconsistent"; differential dropout, small n, missing C-BARQ values, owner-reported outcomes.

**S21. Dog owners' interaction styles (Vienna Clever Dog Lab), Front Psychol 2016.** SECTIONS (abstract and design).

- 220 owner-dog dyads (187 F), 8 standardised situations, three factors (Warmth, Social Support, Control), related to owner age and personality and to dogs' reaction in a threatening approach (warmer owners had proximity-seeking dogs). No training outcomes.
- Engine: #34 supports no rule; NOTES' "associated with outcomes" should read "associated with dog reaction to a threat".

**S22. Affect, activity and home-alone (Front Vet Sci 2021;8:673407).** SECTIONS (abstract).

- n=19 owners, two activity trackers worn 2 weeks; owner-rated affect at departure/return correlated with dog activity. Correlational (rs about 0.24-0.32), tiny, unusually active owners, remote monitoring in week 2 may have changed behaviour, no welfare threshold. No rule.

**S23. Shih HY et al. 2021. Collar vs back-connection harness pulling. Front Vet Sci 8:735680.** SECTIONS.

- 52 RSPCA Queensland shelter dogs (13 mo-11 y, 16-43 kg), within-subject, leash-tension metre, dogs lured toward food or toy. Food lure: harness vs collar maximal tension 1.72 vs 0.71 kgf (p=0.05), mean 0.54 vs 0.32 (p=0.0099), proportion of time pulling 0.67 vs 0.35 (p=0.031). Toy lure: no difference. Stress behaviours no difference, but dogs looked at the experimenter more in harness/food. Authors: only mildly-moderately aroused dogs, shelter dogs only.
- 2nd pass: each test was a **10-second** lure; trial order randomised and significant (tension, pulling time and lip licking fell in the 2nd trial); toy test always after food test; authors read the lip licking as food anticipation; one-handed leash. Engine: supports A22 "warn that back-clip harness will not fix pulling", but on brief lure-driven tests only.

**S24. Johnson & Wynne (first author per Cavalli 2025 ref 58). Comparing efficacy in reducing pulling and welfare impacts of four types of leash walking equipment. PeerJ 2024 (PMC11512810).** SECTIONS.

- n=23 shelter dogs (26 recruited, 13-42 kg), 5-min walks on each of martingale (baseline), front-connection harness, polymer prong, metal prong, strain-gauge, fixed order of increasing "aversiveness", first author as sole handler, >=2 h rests. Pulling impulse differed (chi2=30.6, p<0.001): dogs pulled significantly more on the martingale than on every other device (harness Z=-3.69, Starmark Z=-3.62, prong Z=-3.92); no differences among the other three; trend prong least. Only lip licking (martingale vs harness, p=0.008, r=0.55), looking and sniffing analysable. Authors: no clear evidence of poorer welfare by equipment type; caution generalising; order effect acknowledged. 2nd pass: walks were handler-neutral (handler followed the dog), 4 walks = 20 min total/day, dogs excluded if too excitable to fit equipment (3 of 26), sniffing and gaze are ambiguous welfare indicators, trend for most looking on the harness; authors note any equipment can be aversive (harness restricts shoulder/gait) and that results may not hold with handler tension on typical walks.
- Engine: supports "front-connection harness reduces pulling relative to a tightening collar" (A22). It is NOT support for the "never recommend prong" rule; that rests on AVSAB/China/Vieira (policy). NOTES "no device eliminated pulling" is not something the paper tested.

**S25. Cavalli C, Protopopova A. 2025. Review of collars, harnesses and head collars for walking dogs. Animals 15(15):2162, doi:10.3390/ani15152162 (PMC12345489).** SECTIONS.

- Narrative review: 21 full-text + 2 abstracts (kinematics, pressure, stress behaviour). Conclusions: no universal device; non-tightening front-clip harness best balance for pullers; flat collar for non-pullers; tightening harness, martingale, head collar "greater discomfort... caution"; back-clip harness "suitable" for brachycephalic dogs or non-pullers (esp. chest-strap or Y-shaped); collar pulling raises intraocular pressure and neck pressure.
- 2nd pass: flat collars raise intraocular and neck pressure when pulling (also on a loose leash in brachycephalic/dolichocephalic dogs): review states "neck collars should not be used on brachycephalic dogs"; padding can concentrate pressure; front-clip/Y-shaped harnesses alter elbow/shoulder gait most while chest-strap/Y-shaped designs are "likely the least restrictive" overall; no harness-vs-collar stress difference; Shih/Bailey: back-clip harness yields more pulling. Engine: supports A22 (but note the gait trade-off of front-clip harnesses and the brachycephalic collar caution). Missing from A22: brachycephalic/back-clip exception, and "martingale" caution.

**S26. Proschowsky HF et al. EAAP 2025 slides, "How do Danish dog owners use cages?"** FULL.

- Convenience social-media survey: 2,463 cage users (+119 non-users); 29% had not trained dog before using the cage. Cites Bleuer-Elsner & Masson **2024** (1,305 owners, 23 countries, 25.4% use cages, 5.4 h/day, 4.9 days/wk).
- Engine: descriptive only. The 4.25 h average is a usage mean, not a harm threshold (A27 and A25 ">4 h" are conventions).

**S27. Purdue Canine Welfare Science Center (Croney 2023). Canine body language chart.** FULL (2 pages).

- Green/yellow/red categories: red includes tense/stiff, freezing, hiding, tucked or slow-wagging tail, dilated pupils, whale eye, panting, trembling, lip licking, head-turning, yawning-type behaviours; "Calming signals mean your dog is unsure... If the dog also seems fearful, stop the interaction right away". Slow tail wag can accompany fear. Not validated; no sensitivity data.
- Engine: supports section 5 red-flag vocabulary; the "1 signal = ease, >=2 = stop" rule is engineering convention, not from Purdue.

## 2. Corrections (claim in NOTES / LINKS / README vs what the full text supports)

C1. **">5 instructions hurt outcomes" (NOTES 1, 2, A3, B11; README §4)**. Source: Takeuchi 2000 abstract "Dogs whose owners received more than five instructions were less likely to improve or be cured" (52 dogs with separation anxiety, phone follow-up 6-64 months, observational). Sargisson 2014 turns it into "limited to no more than five separate instructions [33]"; Feuerbacher 2020 restates it as "compliance dropped"; Daniels 2023 says "five or fewer instructions have been recommended". Fix: describe as one observational association about separation-anxiety discharge instructions, widely repeated as a recommendation; not an RCT; severity confounding is plausible. A3 ("max 5 actionable instructions per week incl. management") is a conversion of that unit to a weekly plan and should be tagged low-med. Better-supported adjacent evidence: LAIR (plan complexity rho -0.48). Second-hand wording of Takeuchi varies (Babington 2019 writes "fewer than five"; Sargisson/Feuerbacher "more than five"), and Babington reports Takeuchi found compliance itself _not_ associated with SRB reduction, so even the compliance->outcome link is shaky in that cohort. Takeuchi compliance numbers via Babington: 69% applied counter-conditioning (chew toy), 35% uncoupled cues.
C2. **Session length (NOTES A2, README §3)**. "Hard max 5 min" and "experienced adult up to 5 min" are not in the Fear Free handout (3 min guideline; experienced dogs "capable of longer"). Fear Free = no data. Tag convention.
C3. **Demant DOI (LINKS #2)**: `10.1016/j.applanim.2011.05.009` is wrong (it is Meagher et al., mink). Correct: `10.1016/j.applanim.2011.05.010`. Also note the study counts _sessions_, equal totals per dog.
C4. **Spacing wording (NOTES 1, A5, README §5)**: "weekly single sessions acquired better than daily" is per-session efficiency. Meyer & Ladewig 2008: weekly group needed fewer sessions; 5x/week group finished in fewer days. Demant: same session count per dog. So spacing does not speed calendar time. Demant abstract (now seen): every dog had 18 sessions; weekly-single best, daily-3-in-a-row worst, daily-single ~ weekly-3-in-a-row; the "several times a week" group I earlier cited came from a popular article and is not in the abstract. "3-5 days/week per skill is sufficient" and README "5-6 training days/week" have no study; the "daily short practice acceptable" claim is not tested either (Demant retention only).
C5. **Kis 2017 (LINKS #1, NOTES 1, A6, README §5)**: LINKS says "dogs that slept (or walked) in the hour after training showed gains at 1-week retest; extra learning gave no gain". Paper: Sleep, Walk **and Play** improved at 1 week; the Learn group did not; no group improved at the immediate retest; Play fell at the immediate retest. NOTES' "dogs that ... played did not gain at the immediate retest" is true but omits that they did gain at 1 week. Exp.1 (n=15) has no awake control, so the 3-h post-training improvement is not attributable to sleep.
C6. **Attribution of 64% / 20-30% (LINKS #16, NOTES owner side)**: NOTES "~64% overall and 20-30% when lifestyle change is needed [quoting human/AAHA data]". Paper: 64% overall and 20-30% "for certain treatments" are the **AAHA veterinary** study; human medicine: ~60% average, ~30% for complex or lifestyle-change regimens.
C7. **LAIR predictors (NOTES owner side)**: LAIR outcome was client-rated treatment _success_, not adherence. Retained predictors were negative-direction items: lack of trust OR 2.09, previously-tried recommendations OR 2.64, distress to the animal over time OR 8.05 (2.39-27.11). "Co-created plan" and "feeling not judged" come from Daniels, not LAIR. LAIR explicitly found time/resources/integration items **not** predictive; this undercuts the weight put on time budgets.
C8. **#18 attribution (LINKS)**: PMC7521022 is Sargisson RJ 2014 (Vet Med Res Rep 5:143-151, doi:10.2147/VMRR.S60424). "Sherman & Mills 2014 (approx.)... author attribution to be double-checked" -> Sargisson alone (Sherman & Mills 2008 is cited inside it). Also NOTES "A food-stuffed toy was the instruction owners most reliably followed [#18]" is Herron et al. as reviewed; the same text says it "did not have a significant effect on the incidence" of SRPB and none of the advice prevented SRPB.
C9. **Recall dose (NOTES 1)**: "two sessions/day over 5 days, ~28 min of video per day ... within ~1 m of trainer" is not what China 2020 says: "up to 150 min of training over 5 days" (about 30 min/day); video analysed from "the two training sessions on the first, third and fifth day"; dogs were within 1 m about 70% of the time and >5 m <5% of time in all groups on 10 m leads. LINKS "RCT-like, A-" is too high: Control 2 was trained 4 months later, matched not randomised, with different trainers. Downgrade to B.
C10. **PeerJ 2024 (LINKS #6, NOTES)**: not "efficacy of prong/martingale/harness etc." generically: n=23 shelter dogs, fixed order, one handler; dogs pulled _more_ on the martingale than on front harness or either prong collar; no differences among the other three; "no clear evidence of poorer welfare" for prongs. NOTES "no device eliminated pulling" was not tested. First authors Johnson & Wynne.
C11. **#7 citation**: Cavalli C, Protopopova A. Animals 2025;15(15):2162; doi:10.3390/ani15152162 (LINKS gives no authors/DOI). It is a narrative review of 21 full-text articles + 2 abstracts.
C12. **Owner-reported problems (NOTES, LINKS #24)**: "pulling and recall problems top the owner-reported list" -> barking (7.9-13.4%) and jumping (5.2-7.9%) top at every timepoint; pulling 2.7-6.6%, recall 2.8-6.6%. The 42.1% at 12 months is "any problem". Free-text reporting.
C13. **Crate use 67% -> 36% (NOTES, LINKS #26)**: this is "slept in a kennel/crate in the last 7 days" (66.6% -> 36.3%, multiple response), not crate use for management.
C14. **Crate duration (LINKS #36)**: "~4+ h/day" = weighted mean 4.25 h/day, 23.1 days/month among 2,463 cage users; Bleuer-Elsner 5.4 h/day; 234 dogs >8 h/day. These are usage descriptions; A27/A25 ">4 h" thresholds are conventions, not risk thresholds. #37 year is 2024; survey ran Jan-Jun 2021.
C15. **#4 mislabel**: not a critique; Krahn et al. 2024 tested pre-session discrimination training and found no change in judgement-bias "optimism" (16 dogs). NOTES' "bias tests can reflect learning history" is the authors' motivation, not their finding.
C16. **#43 mislabel**: probable correct reference is Fountain et al. (AABS 282, 2025), 27 studies; "Cameron/Redgate/Zulch 2023" unverified. "Sniff duration has no evidence base" is consistent with its gap statement (secondary only).
C17. **Habit formation (NOTES B8, B9; LINKS #38)**: 66 days is the central value (sources disagree whether median or mean) of the 39 of 96 participants with good curve fit (82 had enough data), students doing self-chosen simple daily behaviours for 84 days; range 18-254 with upper end extrapolated; about half had not plateaued by day 84; exercise ~91 d vs 59-65 d, complexity effect not significant (p=0.328). NOTES B8 "a single missed day did not matter" is right but incomplete: _many_ missed repeats lowered the plateau reached, so "streaks reset nothing" should be "one miss is harmless; repeated misses matter". C18. **Implementation intentions d~0.65 (NOTES #39)**: correct as the 2006 figure; later bias-corrected synthesis reports much smaller (about 0.15-0.35, secondary). Say "positive, probably smaller than 0.65".
C19. **Rehn & Keeling (LINKS #21)**: 12 dogs, 0.5/2/4 h; what changed was the _greeting_, not behaviour while alone; it is not welfare harm. "Stronger greeting/activity at return already after 2 h" is OK; do not use as support for any hours cap.

## 4. Gaps and open questions

1. No study gives a dose (minutes/day, sessions/week) for loose-leash, recall, settle, alone-time or house-training in pet dogs. All numeric defaults are convention and must stay in tunable constants.
2. Spacing evidence = two beagle-lab studies (n=18, n=44) on sessions-to-criterion; no pet-dog field data; nothing on 3-5 days/week.
3. Whether instruction count causally matters (Takeuchi 2000 is observational, abstract only here). A full-text check of Takeuchi (not retrievable here) would clarify how "instructions" were counted.
4. No owner-completion benchmarks for dog-training tasks; Dogs Trust (course completion) and human adherence (60-64%) are proxies. LAIR suggests time is not the main driver; telemetry should test the 0.6 factor.
5. Alone-time hours threshold: none. Rehn & Keeling only show greeting changes; crate hours are descriptive.
6. Sniffing/scent dose: Duranton n=20; scoping review (Fountain 2025) still to be read.
7. House-training and crate methods: no controlled evidence in this folder.
8. Still not obtained in full text: full Takeuchi 2000 (abstract/second-hand only), Lally full text, Demant/Meyer & Ladewig full text and group sizes, Rehn & Keeling full text, Blackwell 2008 full text, Duranton & Horowitz and Krahn abstracts verbatim, Ziv, Grainger and Rooney & Cowan full texts (abstract-level only), Bleuer-Elsner & Masson journal reference, the origin of the 5-min rule. Second pass obtained: Demant abstract, Babington thesis, Fountain review, Sleep Foundation page.
9. Meyer & Ladewig 2008 should be added to LINKS; consider adding Herron et al. 2009 (stuffed toy and shelter-adopter advice) as the primary for the "followed but ineffective" claim.
10. Habit/implementation-intention evidence is human, general, mostly daily solitary behaviours; dog-owner transfer untested.
